Pipeline

From pixels to people counts

How roughly 1.4 million Street View images become calibrated counts of women, men and children on Jakarta's streets. One track finds and classifies people in four stages. A second, independent track measures what the street itself is made of.

The whole system at a glance

Diagram scrolls sideways →

The two tracks share the same images but never feed into each other. Segmentation describes the street; it plays no part in counting people.

Classifying people from Street View is several different problems at once. The system has to find small, often occluded people in cluttered scenes. It has to set aside motorbike riders in a city where motorbikes dominate the road. And it has to judge gender and life stage from body shape and clothing alone, because faces are blurred in Street View imagery. Each of these needs different features and has a different amount of training data. So each runs as its own stage, trained and checked on its own. When one stage has to change, only that stage and the ones after it are re-run.

One design rule runs through all of it: store raw signals and apply thresholds as late as possible. Detection confidences, classifier scores and box sizes are all saved at inference time. Every cut-off is applied afterwards, to the stored table. That lets the calibration be refitted, or a filter changed, without re-running inference on 1.4 million images.

Stage 0

Detection, tuned for recall

YOLO26x, a large COCO-pretrained detector, runs on every image at its longest side of 640 px. It is set deliberately loose: confidence 0.22, NMS IoU 0.30. The costs are lopsided. A false positive, such as a mannequin or a poster, can still be removed by a later stage. A missed person is gone for good and lowers the count directly.

The same pass also records cars, motorbikes, bicycles, buses and trucks. These aren't analysed in their own right. They feed the geometric rider test in Stage 1.

Stage 1

The rider gate

The study measures people present in public space. Its hand-labelling protocol excludes anyone travelling on a moving motorised vehicle, and records people on bicycles separately. In Jakarta, a large share of the people the detector finds are on motorbikes. The rider gate brings the machine count in line with that definition. It is the largest single filter in the pipeline.

The two tests fail in different ways. Geometry misses riders whose vehicle the detector didn't box, for example because it is hidden behind other traffic. The classifier can catch those from the person's pose and framing alone.

The classifier is a binary EfficientNet-B0 at 224 × 224, trained for 40 epochs and selected on balanced accuracy, with the rider class up-weighted. The interesting part is its negative class. Pedestrian examples weren't sampled at random. They were drawn from capped pools of deliberately hard cases: people standing next to vehicles, unusual poses, mannequins and posters, people seen through car windows, and tiny partial-body crops. Random crops covering 60–100% of each training image simulate the small detections seen in panoramas. The 0.20 threshold was chosen on out-of-fold predictions and is deliberately low.

Eligibility

Which crops can be classified at all

Some detections are too uncertain or too small to say anything about gender or age. These rules run on the stored table, first match wins. Excluded detections stay in the data with a label saying why, so the funnel can be audited later.

Rule 1

low_conf

Detector confidence below 0.50. Detection ran at 0.22 for recall; classification needs more certainty that a person is there.

Rule 2

bad_quality

Crop height outside 40–180 px, or a crop that is blurred, dark or fragmentary. Too little body visible to judge.

Rule 3

rider

Flagged by the Stage 1 gate.

Crops that survive are cut with a 15% margin on each side, resized to 224 × 224 and ImageNet-normalised before scoring.

Stage 2

Two classifiers, built differently

EfficientNet-B3 asks two independent yes/no questions of each crop through two sigmoid heads on a shared backbone: is this a woman? and is this a child? YOLO11m-cls asks one three-way question, woman, man or child, through a softmax that forces the answers to compete. The two models make different structural assumptions, so they give the calibration step two different views of each crop.

No threshold is applied at this stage. Both models' raw scores are stored for every crop.

Training: public benchmarks, then Jakarta

Public pedestrian-attribute datasets come from fixed surveillance cameras in East Asia and Europe. Jakarta's Street View imagery differs in viewpoint, light, clothing, head coverings and crowd density. EfficientNet-B3 was trained in two sessions:

  • Session 1PA-100K only, woman vs man, 12 epochs, lr 3e-4, early backbone blocks frozen.
  • Session 2From Session 1's weights, 35 epochs at lr 3e-5, on a mix of PA-100K, PETA (women and children) and hand-labelled Jakarta Street View crops (woman / man / child / unidentified).

Women are under-represented in the source data, so training deliberately boosts them through a weighted sampler, focal loss and checkpoint selection on female F1. The result is a training set roughly half female among adults. On Jakarta's streets, women are about a quarter of identified adults. That gap between training and deployment priors is the main reason raw scores can't be read as probabilities, and why Stage 3 exists.

Stage 3

Calibration: expected counts, not forced labels

The obvious approach is to threshold each crop into "woman" or "man" and count the labels. That throws away uncertainty, and it misbehaves at scale. Many locations have only a few borderline detections, and a hard threshold can turn all of them into zero women.

Instead, the classifier scores are calibrated jointly over three mutually exclusive classes, woman, man and child, using Dirichlet calibration (Kull et al., 2019). It is fitted on a separate hand-labelled audit of detected crops, reweighted to match the production data. Each eligible detection gets three probabilities that sum to one. A panorama's expected count of women is the sum of its detections' woman-probabilities, and the same for men and children.

Why not hard labels?

Two alternatives are kept as robustness checks: a two-class isotonic calibration, and hard labels from fixed decision thresholds. In the study's analysis of streets around new public spaces, all three give the same direction of result. But hard labels assign no woman at all to many locations, and in a count model those locations drop out. Under hard labels the estimates for women rest on about half the observations that the calibrated counts use.

What calibration can't fix. Calibration improves classification of people who were detected. It can't recover people the detector missed or the gates removed. That's why the study takes its headline levels from hand-labelled imagery and uses the machine counts for relative variation across space and time. How far detection error goes is covered on the Validation page.

Challenges we caught

Integrity

Keeping each score attached to the right person

In images with several people, scores were at first keyed by image rather than by detection, so people in the same frame could end up sharing a score. Every box now carries its own index from detection onwards, the affected stages were re-run, and a standing check rejects any score file where two boxes share a result.

Integrity

A lookup that returns the wrong thing is worse than a crash

Images are stored by heading, as 0.jpg, 60.jpg and so on. An index keyed on the bare filename quietly resolved different panoramas to the same file, with no error raised. It is now keyed on the full dataset, year, panorama and heading path.

Parallel track

Streetscape segmentation

A YOLOv8x-seg model trained on Mapillary Vistas runs on the same images. Mapillary Vistas was chosen for its geographic diversity. The model produces the share of each image covered by 28 streetscape categories, including sidewalk, curb, crosswalk, road, building, vegetation and street furniture. These features describe the street environment. They play no part in counting people. Full results are on the Segmentation page.
