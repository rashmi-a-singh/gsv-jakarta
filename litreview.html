Literature

Related work

Three strands of computer-vision research this pipeline builds on, and where Jakarta's imagery pushes past what they were designed for.

Strand 1

Pedestrian detection and attribute recognition

Single-pass detection has advanced steadily since YOLOv3 established one-shot bounding-box regression, through to the anchor-free heads of recent YOLO versions. Crowd benchmarks such as CrowdHuman and CityPersons pushed performance under occlusion. Attribute benchmarks such as PA-100K and PETA made gender and age classification from person crops feasible at scale. The common limitation is the data: these benchmarks come from fixed surveillance cameras and dash-cams in East Asian and European cities. Even the first demonstration that Street View could replace manual pedestrian counts (Yin et al., 2015) was tested in low-density US cities. Dense motorbike traffic, tropical light and head coverings, all at once, are outside what this literature was built on. That combination is what the rider gate and the Jakarta fine-tuning are for.

Strand 2

Street-level imagery for urban analysis

Biljecki & Ito's (2021) review of 619 papers maps how street-level imagery is used across urban analytics. It identifies deep learning as the dominant method, but flags pedestrian attribute recognition as underdeveloped and Global South cities as sparsely covered. Gebru et al. (2017) inferred neighbourhood demographics from 50 million Street View images of cars across US cities. Green-view and streetscape studies established segmentation as a way to measure the built environment. Mapillary Vistas (Neuhold et al., 2017), which trains this project's segmentation model, made geographically diverse street-scene segmentation possible. This project applies both, people and streetscape, at city scale in a tropical Global South megacity.

Strand 3

Domain adaptation

Pan & Yang's (2010) taxonomy describes this pipeline's setting: the source data (PA-100K, PETA) and target data (Jakarta Street View) share a task but not a distribution. The leading unsupervised methods, DANN (Ganin et al., 2016), ADDA (Tzeng et al., 2017) and CORAL (Sun & Saenko, 2016), align feature spaces without target labels, but assume a modest domain gap. GeoNet (Kalluri et al., 2023) shows that assumption strains even across geographies with similar cameras. Jakarta's gap is wider still: surveillance footage to panoramic street imagery, temperate to tropical light, and different clothing and head coverings. So this project uses supervised adaptation instead. The model is pre-trained on PA-100K and then fine-tuned on a mix of benchmark data and hand-labelled Jakarta crops. Calibration then corrects the remaining mismatch between training and deployment priors. This trades annotation effort for reliability on a target domain the standard tools weren't built for.
